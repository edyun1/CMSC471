CMSC471: Introduction to Data Visualization · Spring 2026 · Ed Yun and Andrew Zhang

Dataset and Background

We used the OECD Greenhouse Gas Emissions dataset, which contains per capita greenhouse gas emissions data across roughly 90+ countries from 2014 to 2023. The metric is total greenhouse gas emissions excluding land use, land-use change, and forestry (LULUCF), expressed in kilograms of CO₂-equivalent per person (kg CO₂e/person). The dataset covers all major greenhouse gases.

One important characteristic of this dataset is that not all countries report data for every year. Some countries have complete coverage across 2014–2023 while others only appear in a handful of years. The dataset also contains regional aggregates like EU27_2020, OECDA, and OECDSO that are not individual countries, as well as small city-states like VAT (Vatican City) whose per capita figures are statistically meaningless at a global/national scale. These features influenced the design decisions for both visualizations.

Our visualization aims to answer: which countries emit the most greenhouse gases per person, and how does each country compare to the global average for a given year?

White Hat Visualization

White Hat Write-up

The white hat visualization is a horizontal bar chart showing per capita greenhouse gas emissions by country for a selected year. Each bar is colored using a sequential blue-to-orange gradient where warmer colors indicate higher emissions, giving the reader two visual channels — bar length and color — that reinforce the same information. Bars are sorted in descending order so the highest emitters are immediately visible at the top without requiring the reader to scan the whole chart. A reference line marking the dataset average is included so readers can visually benchmark any country without doing mental math, and every bar is labeled with its exact value. The X-axis is clearly titled with the full unit (kg CO₂-equivalent per person) so there is no ambiguity about what is being measured.

Several entries were excluded with full transparency: VAT (Vatican City), a city-state of roughly 800 people whose per capita figure is statistically meaningless at a global scale, and the regional aggregates EU27_2020, OECDA, OECDSO, and OECD, which represent grouped averages rather than individual countries and would be misleading to compare alongside national figures. All other countries present in the dataset for the selected year are shown. The year filter was implemented as a single-value dropdown rather than a range selector. This was a deliberate design choice — because different countries report data for different years, allowing a range selection would silently sum unequal amounts of data across countries and produce a misleading comparison. Forcing the reader to pick one specific year ensures every country shown is measured on equal footing. The default is set to 2019, which has strong country coverage across the dataset.

To go beyond simple filtering and sorting, we created a custom calculated field called Deviation from Average, defined as each country's emissions value minus the average across all countries for the selected year. This computed field appears in the tooltip on hover, so when a reader mouses over any bar they can see both the raw emissions value and exactly how far above or below the global average that country sits. This gives the reader a meaningful point of comparison without cluttering the main chart with extra numbers.

Black Hat Visualization

Black Hat Write-up

The black hat visualization uses the same OECD dataset but applies a series of intentional design decisions that distort the reader's perception in ways that are difficult to detect at a glance. The most significant deception is invisible — instead of filtering to a single year like the white hat viz, this chart sums all reported years from 2014 to 2023 into one combined value per country with no disclosure that this is happening. Because countries report data for different numbers of years, this means countries with fewer reporting years appear to have dramatically lower emissions than countries that reported consistently. A country like Saudi Arabia ends up looking far lower than it actually is for any given year purely because it reported less frequently. The reader has no way of knowing this since there is no year label, no note about aggregation, and no methodology explanation anywhere on the chart.

On top of that, the chart silently filters to only show countries above a certain emissions threshold (keeping only major emitters), removing all low-emitting countries from view entirely without any explanation. We also made the X-axis start at 18 rather than 0 making it seem like the lower end of high emitters are very close to nothing when in reality they are far above 0. The outliers VAT (Vatican City) and BRN (Brunei) are deliberately kept in the dataset, which forces the color gradient to stretch all the way to their extreme values and compresses most real countries into a narrow band of similar-looking blue bars, making meaningful differences between them nearly invisible. The X-axis is labeled "Emissions Value" — a broad label that tells the reader absolutely nothing about units or what is actually being measured. The title omits the year, the unit, and any methodology. A color legend labeled "Obs Value" adds visual noise without explaining anything. Finally, a median and an average reference line are displayed simultaneously, calculated on the already manipulated and unevenly aggregated dataset, adding a false appearance of analytical rigor while actually contributing to clutter and confusion. Along with quartiles that add clutter and do not help as the data is already being ruined by outliers.

Development Process

We worked together as a pair on this project just in-person at Mckeldin. We would say basically just split the work evenly as we did all of Tableau on one laptop together and split the webpage portion fairly. A significant portion of time was spent understanding the structure of the OECD dataset and figuring out what each column actually meant, since the CSV uses abbreviated ISO country codes and dense data columns that are not immediately readable. Building both visualizations in Tableau Public took additional time, particularly getting the color encoding, reference line, year dropdown, and Deviation from Average calculated field working correctly for the white hat viz. Also coming up with creative ways for both white hat and black hat aspects proved to be a little challenging as we never had to think this way. Writing the write-ups and putting together the webpage added time on top of that. We had trouble getting the visuals to show correctly and separately. Overall the project took approximately 7–9 hours.

AI Usage

We used Claude throughout this project. Claude helped us read and understand the OECD dataset early on — for example, explaining that entries like VAT, BRN, EU27_2020, OECDA, and OECDSO were not individual countries but a city-state, a small outlier nation, and regional aggregates, and explaining why mixing these with individual countries in a visualization would be misleading. Claude also clarified that the three-letter ISO country codes used in the dataset could be easy to confuse (for example AUS is Australia, not Austria, which is AUT). Claude helped us understand why different countries had different numbers of data points across years and how summing across those unequal reporting periods without disclosure produces a misleading comparison — which we then intentionally exploited for the black hat visualization. Claude also provided guidance throughout the webpage build process and helped clean up and structure the webpage code to have Tableau visuals within it.

Major design decisions, the choice of deceptive techniques, and the overall framing of both visualizations were developed and decided by us.
